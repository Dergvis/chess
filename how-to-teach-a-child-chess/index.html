How to Teach a Child Chess

Start at 4–6 years old in four steps: the board, one piece at a time, checkmate, a first game. Keep lessons to 10–15 minutes. Practise free in CHEZZIES.

START ADVENTURE↗

  1. Meet the board

    Show that the board has 64 squares and that the bottom-right square is light. Set up the pieces together, one type at a time, and name each one.

    5 minutes, once

  2. One piece at a time

    Introduce one piece per session. Play a mini game with only that piece: for example, only pawns, and whoever gets a pawn to the far side first wins. Add the next piece when the first feels easy.

    1–2 weeks

  3. Check and checkmate

    Explain that the goal is to trap the king. Start with simple positions: a king and rook against a lone king. Ask “Where can the king go?” before every move.

    1–2 weeks

  4. The first full game

    Play a whole game slowly and talk out loud. It is fine to take a move back while learning. After the game, find one good move your child made.

    from week 3–5

What age should a child start chess?

There is no single right age. Many children start between 4 and 6. A good sign of readiness is when your child can recognise the pieces, follow a simple rule such as “the rook only goes straight”, and stay with one small task for about ten minutes. Younger children, around 3, can enjoy the pieces as characters and play simple games with one or two pieces. Older beginners of 7–10 usually learn the full rules in a few weeks. If your child loses interest, put the board away for a week and try again; it is a game, not a test.

How do you teach a toddler chess?

With toddlers, forget full games. Use the pieces as toys with names and stories: the rook is a tower that rolls straight, the bishop slides on its own colour, the knight jumps. Play “catch the pawn” with one rook against two pawns. Keep each activity to 5–10 minutes and let your child lead. The goal at this age is to love the pieces, not to win.

What does a child practise when playing chess?

Chess gives children regular practice in a few everyday skills. Waiting for your turn and thinking before acting. Looking at a situation from someone else’s side: what does my opponent want? Following rules and accepting the result of a fair game. Planning two or three steps ahead, and noticing when a plan does not work. Counting, comparing and recognising patterns on the board. None of this needs long lessons: short, regular games are where it happens. Just as important, chess is something a parent and child can enjoy together at the same table, on equal terms, with no screen required for the board version.

What do you need to start?

Very little. A chessboard and pieces of any kind, or a free chess game in the browser on a computer, tablet or phone. A quiet table and 10–15 minutes. One rule or one piece to learn today. For you as a parent: a short guide to how the pieces move, open on your phone, so you can check a rule in a few seconds instead of guessing. That is enough for the first months.

What can you skip at the beginning?

Leave castling, en passant, chess notation, openings and the clock for later. None of them is needed to play and enjoy the first games. Introduce castling when your child plays full games comfortably, and en passant when it actually happens on the board. Point values (pawn 1, knight 3, bishop 3, rook 5, queen 9) can wait until your child starts asking which trades are good.

Should I let my child win?

Not by playing badly on purpose; children notice. Instead, give a fair head start: play without your queen, or with only pawns and a king. Make the handicap smaller as your child improves. Real wins teach more and feel better.

How do I keep my child interested?

Do not correct every move; pick one thing per game. Praise good thinking, not only good results: “You saw that my bishop was attacking your knight.” Play at the same time a few days a week so it becomes a habit. Let your child explain a move to you; teaching is the fastest way to learn. And stop before your child gets tired.

How long does it take a child to learn chess?

Learning how the pieces move usually takes a few weeks of short sessions. Playing a full game with all the main rules often takes one to three months. Getting good takes years, and that is fine: the useful skills, such as patience, planning and looking at the other side’s ideas, start growing from the first lessons.

Can a parent who does not play chess teach it?

Yes. You only need to stay one step ahead. Learn each piece just before you show it, use a short guide such as how chess pieces move, and let a game do part of the work. In CHEZZIES the board checks every move, so you do not need to be the referee.

How do you teach a 4 or 5 year old chess?

Keep the same four steps, but move more slowly and use more play. At 4–5, children learn best with one piece and one small goal: “Can your rook reach the star?” Put a small toy or coin on a square and let your child move the piece there. Celebrate every success. Use stories: the pawns are brave soldiers who never step back, the king is old and walks slowly. A full game can wait until your child knows all the moves and enjoys the mini games, often a few months later.

How do you teach a 6 to 8 year old chess?

Children of 6–8 can usually learn all the moves in a few weeks and start full games soon after. They enjoy rules and fairness, so explain why a rule exists: castling keeps the king safe, promotion rewards a pawn for a long journey. At this age, short puzzles work very well: one position, one question, one answer. Ask your child to say what the other side is threatening before every move. Children of 9–10 can follow longer explanations and enjoy comparing their games with yours.

What mini games can you play before a full game?

Pawn war: each side has only its eight pawns, and whoever gets a pawn to the far row first wins. Rook against pawns: one rook tries to capture three pawns before they reach the end. Knight jumps: put a knight in a corner and count how many jumps it needs to reach the opposite corner. Treasure hunt: place a coin on a square and find the fastest way for each piece to reach it. King and rook against king: the first checkmate every child should learn. Each mini game takes 3–5 minutes and teaches one idea.

How do you explain check and checkmate to a child?

Say it simply. Check: “Your king is in danger. You must save him right now.” There are three ways to save him: move the king, block the attack with another piece, or capture the attacking piece. Checkmate: “The king is in danger and none of the three ways works. The game is over.” Then show it on the board with only a few pieces, and let your child find the escape squares with a finger. Stalemate can come later: if the player to move has no legal move and is not in check, the game is a draw.

How do you teach a child to think before moving?

Give your child one short question to ask before every move: “What does my opponent want to do?” Later add a three-step check that many coaches use: checks, captures, threats. Is there a check I can give? Is there a piece I can capture safely? What is my opponent threatening? Saying it out loud for the first weeks makes it a habit. If your child moves too fast, try a rule: hands on the knees until you have found your move.

What if my child gets upset after losing?

It is normal, especially for young children. Name the feeling: “You are sad because you lost the queen.” Take a short break, then look at the game together and find one move that was good. Shake hands at the end of every game, win or lose, so it becomes part of the ritual. Shorter games, fair head starts and puzzles where every attempt can succeed help a lot. Never force another game when your child wants to stop.

How often should a child play chess?

A few short sessions a week work better than one long one. Two or three sessions of 10–15 minutes are a good start for young children; older children may want more as they get interested. Consistency matters more than length: the same day and time helps chess become a calm, familiar routine. Days off are fine. If chess starts to feel like homework, make the sessions shorter and add more mini games.

Should my child join a chess club or get a coach?

There is no rush. Many children happily learn at home for months or years. A club can help when your child plays full games, enjoys them and asks for new opponents; meeting other children is often the best motivation. A coach is useful when your child wants to improve and you no longer know what to teach next. Until then, playing together, short puzzles and a game that checks every move are more than enough.
